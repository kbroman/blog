common variable immunodeficiency

2025-11-27

This will be yet another long post about my health. I guess this is what happens when you hit your 50s.

This summer I was diagnosed with Common Variable Immunodeficiency (CVID), an immune disorder where my B cells have basically stopped producing antibodies, which leads to frequent infections (particularly sinus infections and pneumonia) as well as the potential for numerous other complications. CVID is the most common primary immune deficiency, but it is still quite rare (about 1/25,000, suggesting there are maybe 250 affected people in Wisconsin, and fewer than 14,000 in the US). And it’s quite heterogeneous (hence “variable”): many people with CVID just have increased respiratory infections, others also have frequent gastrointestinal infections, and many others also have a variety of complications such as lung disease and autoimmune problems.

It seems odd to be diagnosed with an immune deficiency in my late middle-age, but it’s not uncommon for this disease: typical age of diagnosis is 20-50, and there is generally a very long time from the onset of symptoms to diagnosis (on average 5 years). Onset is very hard to define: have I had frequent respiratory infections, and when did they begin? (I had pneumonia back in 1998, at age 29; was that the start?)

diagnosis

My diagnosis came by happenstance. A year ago, routine blood work showed increased liver enzymes (AST/SGOT and ALT/SGPT), and so I was referred to a hepatologist. An ultrasound of the liver showed “increased echogenicity”, so they did a fancier ultrasound (“elastography”, to me it was like an ultrasound with a bunch of shaking) which was normal. The hepatologist suggested taking a vitamin E supplement and repeating the blood work in 4 months.

In the follow-up blood work, the liver enzymes had dipped back into the normal range, but it included an IgG measurement (I think looking to see if it was elevated, which might indicate an infection), which was below the detection limit.

That seemed kind of crazy to me; I figured it must be a lab error. But the retested it, and also measured IgA and IgM, and in the second measurement IgG was again below the detection limit, and IgA was also extremely low, while IgM was normal.

(Interestingly, in that initial blood work, my blood protein level was low, despite the fact that my diet is quite high in protein. My blood protein has been low ever since they first measured it, back in 2020, and it’s low by about the amount of IgG that I should have.)

And so I was referred to an immunologist.

sinus and ear infection

While all this was happening, I’d taking a plane trip to Portland, Oregon, where I caught some sort of respiratory virus (apparently not COVID), and ended up being sick for the entire month of July, including a bad sinus and ear infection that resulted in a ruptured ear drum. It finally cleared up after 10 days of antibiotic ear drops and 3 weeks of oral antibiotics. A trip to ontolaryngology showed the ear drum had healed, but I had reduced hearing in that ear, which they’ll be rechecking in six months.

Before the ruptured ear drum, I had felt like my lungs were affected and so went to urgent care to ask about pneumonia. They did a chest xray but it looked fine.

immune deficiency foundation

The Immune Deficiency Foundation (IDF) has been a great source of information. And there’s a monthly online support group that has been great.

N95 masks and other valuable tools

  • If I’ve learned anything from the COVID pandemic, it’s that we can generally avoid seasonal respiratory infections, and masking is a key and very effective tool for that.
  • I’ve been continuing to mask in public, and generally to avoid in-person events
  • I’ve had covid twice, and the experiences were quite bad. I didn’t get that sick, but I was sick for a very long time, and isolated in my basement that whole time, which was really disruptive
  • I don’t want that to happen again
  • Also air ventilation and filtering, and measuring air ventilation through CO2 monitors: expensive ones, hand-made ones, and tiny portable ones from Australia.
  • Also vaccination

future

It’s been an eventful year for me, health-wise. A year ago, I had a cardiac ablation which solved my PVCs, and earlier this year I had carpal tunnel release surgery on each wrist (the right in April and the left in June).

All that is fixed, but now I’ve got CVID, or more precisely now it’s recognized that I have CVID. My future will include weekly antibody infusions, twice daily sinus rinses, and lots of regular blood work and other diagnostic tests, and visits to all the specialists. All that for the rest of my life (say 30 years).

Don’t lose your health insurance!

Keep up the masking and vaccination, and assessment and improvement of air quality.
